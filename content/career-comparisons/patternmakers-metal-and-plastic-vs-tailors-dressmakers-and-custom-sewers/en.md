# Patternmakers, metal and plastic vs. tailors, dressmakers, and custom sewers

## One word, two kinds of pattern

Metal and plastic patternmakers may build a pattern for forming a casting mold and check it against a drawing. Tailors, dressmakers, and custom sewers may make or adapt a garment pattern and fit the finished garment to a person. Both revise patterns. What they measure and hand over differs.

## Compare the work

| Item | Metal and plastic patternmaker | Tailor, dressmaker, or custom sewer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deliverable | A foundry pattern and dimensional check | An altered or made-to-measure garment and fitting notes |
| Setting to ask about | Pattern shop and contact with molding or foundry teams | Sewing station, fitting room or alterations counter |
| Typical U.S. entry education | High school diploma or equivalent | No formal educational credential in the BLS classification |
| Typical on-the-job training | Moderate-term | Moderate-term |
| 2025 U.S. median annual wage | $58,000 | $41,640 |

Wages cover nonfarm wage and salary workers and exclude self-employment. Pay from an independent alteration business falls outside that coverage. ## Two handoffs for a theater production

A fictional theater orders a cast ornament and a costume. The patternmaker makes the foundry pattern from the ornament drawing, records critical dimensions and submits it for molding review. During the costume fitting, the tailor sees that the wearer cannot comfortably raise an arm for a scene. The tailor confirms the movement with the wearer, revises the garment pattern and schedules a refit. The production lead tracks readiness for molding and for costume delivery separately.

## Getting started in the U.S.

For industrial patternmaking, BLS lists high school or equivalent as typical and moderate-term on-the-job training to build competency. Ask how new workers are taught to read drawings and check patterns. For tailoring, BLS lists no formal educational credential as typical and moderate-term on-the-job training. A sample alteration and fitting notes can show a shop what you can already do. Ask which materials and fittings the employer will train you to handle. [BLS education and training](https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/occupational-projections-and-characteristics.htm)

## Ask how feedback reaches the bench

Ask a pattern shop who receives molding feedback, how revisions are recorded and whether work is new or rework. Ask a tailoring shop who takes measurements, how many fittings a job may need and who handles a late change.

## Moving between the roles

A patternmaker can bring a redacted version log and dimensional checklist, then learn fabric behavior, measurement and fitting. A tailor can bring a redacted alteration record and pattern revision sketch, then practice industrial drawing interpretation, tolerances and foundry handoffs.

## A 30-minute paper exercise

Use two fictional orders. The foundry pattern width must be 59–61 mm and the check reads 62. A fitting note says the costume restricts an arm movement. Garment pattern A keeps the original ease; option B proposes more ease and has not been refitted. Take ten minutes to decide whether the foundry pattern can go to molding, ten to choose which garment option to bring to the mock refit, and ten to write both handoffs. Record who rechecks the dimension and who confirms that the costume works in the refit.
