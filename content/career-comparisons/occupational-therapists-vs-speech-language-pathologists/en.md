# Occupational Therapists vs. Speech-Language Pathologists: Who Checks What When Ordering a Meal?

## What each clinician assesses

An occupational therapist evaluates how a person carries out an activity, including the effects of movement and object placement. A speech-language pathologist evaluates communication difficulties and develops and reviews a treatment plan.

## The same practice order

Imagine a client who wants to order independently at a community cafe. The occupational therapist observes whether moving a picture menu within reach changes the client's ability to point. The speech-language pathologist observes whether the client distinguishes the pictured choices and can convey a selection by pointing or speaking. The two clinicians record menu position, communication method, and any prompts for the next session.

## Getting started in the U.S.

Occupational therapists typically need a master's degree in occupational therapy. Speech-language pathologists typically need at least a master's degree in speech-language pathology. BLS reports that both occupations require licensure in every state. Check the state board for specific requirements. [BLS occupational therapy entry](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/occupational-therapists.htm#tab-4) · [BLS speech-language pathology entry](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/speech-language-pathologists.htm#tab-4)

## Working conditions

Occupational therapy may involve setting up a practice environment. Speech-language pathology may require time to assess, document and talk with caregivers. Ask whether both clinicians can observe the same task and who follows up with staff.

## Moving between roles

Interviewing, observation, and team documentation carry over. Moving into occupational therapy requires training in activity and environment assessment. Moving into speech-language pathology requires training in communication disorders and supervised clinical work. Verify qualifications for the destination role.

## Try both in 30 minutes

Use three fictional notes. The client cannot reach a distant card but points when it is closer. The pictures show rice and noodles. The client points to noodles and says the name aloud. The staff member repeats back “rice.” Spend 10 minutes documenting access, 10 listing communication questions, and 10 drafting a joint handoff that records the conditions.
