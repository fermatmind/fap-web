# Computer Systems Analysts vs. Data Warehousing Specialists on a Stockout Report

## Start with the work

Computer systems analysts find out what users need to do and turn that into requirements and acceptance cases. Data warehousing specialists assemble and verify the records that supply reporting. Both can investigate the same number, but one asks what the number should mean and the other checks how it was produced.

## Compare what you hand off

| Deliverable | Computer Systems Analysts | Data Warehousing Specialists |
|---|---|---|
| Example | Reporting needs, metric definition, and acceptance cases | Source mapping, warehouse loads, and reconciliation |

## A shared case

A fictional retailer wants a daily stockout report. The analyst asks store staff whether reserved stock counts as available and how goods in transit should appear. The warehouse specialist joins inventory records with item data and tracks records that do not match. The two compare a worked example against the report and the source totals.

Ask the employer which education, training and experience count for the specific job.

For systems analysis, document user needs and write criteria that would expose a wrong answer. For warehousing, take two small teaching tables through a load and show the results of the checks. Ask how a specific employer divides requirements and data work.

## What to ask an employer

Systems analysts may spend time resolving different user interpretations. Warehouse specialists work around arrival of source feeds and can have to delay a release when a necessary source is late or incomplete. Ask analysts who resolves competing definitions. Ask warehouse teams how they handle a late source feed and which checks must pass before a report is refreshed.

## Skills that transfer

To move into warehousing, turn a requirement into a source mapping and test it against a sample batch. To move into systems analysis, start with a discrepancy and ask users which decision depends on the figure.

## Try it in 30 minutes

Use a fictional inventory sheet. Item A has four units on hand, all reserved. Item B has none on hand and eight arriving tomorrow. Row C has stock but no item ID. Store staff want to know what they can sell immediately today. Spend fifteen minutes defining the measure and expected results for A and B, and fifteen writing how to handle C and what to check before release.
