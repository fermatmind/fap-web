# Patternmakers, metal and plastic vs. tool and die makers

## What the next operation receives

Metal and plastic patternmakers make and check patterns, including patterns used to form casting molds. Tool and die makers make, fit and repair production tools such as dies, jigs and gauges. Both may machine material and verify drawings. The item's next use is a better guide to the role than the tools at the bench.

## Compare the roles

| Item | Metal and plastic patternmaker | Tool and die maker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work product | A verified pattern or template and dimension record | A fitted die, jig, gauge or tool and inspection record |
| Work setting to ask about | Pattern shop and trial work with the foundry | Toolroom and support for production or trial runs |
| Typical U.S. entry education | High school diploma or equivalent | Postsecondary nondegree award |
| Typical on-the-job training | Moderate-term | Long-term |
| 2025 U.S. median annual wage | $58,000 | $64,050 |

Wages cover nonfarm wage and salary workers and exclude self-employment. ## One casting project, two acceptance records

A fictional shop makes a trial bracket. The patternmaker checks the pattern that will form the casting mold, records a deviation and reworks it before handoff to molding. The tool and die maker checks the fit of a separate die used to trim the casting after it is made. Each submits an inspection record to the trial lead, who authorizes the next step for each operation separately.

## Getting started in the U.S.

For patternmaking, BLS lists high school or equivalent as typical entry education, followed by moderate-term on-the-job training to develop competency. Ask how a new worker progresses from reading drawings to checking and assembling patterns. For tool and die work, a technical school or community college nondegree program can be one entry route. Some workers train through an employer-sponsored apprenticeship that combines paid work and technical instruction. BLS lists long-term on-the-job training for the occupation. [BLS typical preparation](https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/occupational-projections-and-characteristics.htm) · [BLS tool and die training](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/production/machinists-and-tool-and-die-makers.htm#tab-4)

## Ask what happens after a trial

For a pattern job, ask how the shop handles foundry feedback and who owns changes to a reused pattern. For tool and die work, ask who checks repairs, whether you support production at the machine and how toolroom shifts are staffed.

## Moving between the roles

A patternmaker can bring a redacted revision log and dimensional report, then learn tooling fit checks, trial inspection and repair workflows. A tool and die maker can bring a redacted inspection sheet and rework record, then learn pattern construction and the handoff to molding and foundry staff.

## A 30-minute paper exercise

Use only mock records. The pattern's allowed dimension is 99–101 mm and the record shows 100 mm. A separate mock clearance check between two reference faces of the trim die allows 0.20–0.30 mm and shows 0.35 mm. Take ten minutes comparing each result with its own drawing, ten making the two handoffs, and ten writing a release recommendation for the trial lead. Use only the two inspection records. Identify which record supports release and which must be held until the die is adjusted or reworked and the clearance is measured again. The trial lead signs off separately.
