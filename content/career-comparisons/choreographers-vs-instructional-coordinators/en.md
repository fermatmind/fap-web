# Choreographers vs. Instructional Coordinators: Turning a Routine Into a Lesson

## What each role delivers

Choreographers design movement and formations and lead rehearsals. Instructional coordinators work with teachers to develop materials, support instruction and evaluate implementation.

## Compare the work

| Work item | Choreographers | Instructional Coordinators |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Rehearsable movement and formations | Lesson sequence and teacher materials |
| Basis | Movement effect, space and formations | Learning goals, class time and observations |
| U.S. median annual wage (2025) | $55,310 | $77,440 |

## How the work meets

A fictional school uses a short dance phrase in a rhythm unit. The choreographer hands over an eight-count sequence, formations and a simpler turn. The coordinator builds these into two lessons, pilots them with teachers and sends observations about students keeping time back to the choreographer.

## How to enter

BLS lists a high school diploma or equivalent, five or more years of related experience and long-term on-the-job training as typical for choreographers. It lists a master’s degree and five or more years of related experience for instructional coordinators. For a U.S. public school position, ask the state education department whether the job requires a teaching or administrator license. [BLS occupation table](https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/occupational-projections-and-characteristics.htm) [BLS public school licensing](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/education-training-and-library/instructional-coordinators.htm#tab-4)

## Work conditions

Choreography may follow rehearsal and venue schedules. Coordination may follow teacher training, trial lessons and curriculum reviews. Ask who collects observations and revises teacher materials.

## Moving between roles

Moving into coordination, build on movement instruction and add curriculum planning, teacher support and assessment. Moving into choreography, apply demonstration skills and develop movement design and blocking.

## Try it in 30 minutes

A fictional class keeps time through count four but loses the beat on a turn in the last four counts. There are two 20-minute lessons. Spend ten minutes each revising a phrase, planning teacher materials and explaining what feedback goes back to the choreographer.
