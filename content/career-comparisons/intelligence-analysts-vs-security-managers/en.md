# Intelligence Analysts vs. Security Managers: Who Resolves Conflicting Notices?

## What each role delivers

The O*NET occupation focuses on intelligence in law enforcement and public safety. An intelligence analyst corroborates reports and documents confirmed information and gaps in a briefing. A security manager coordinates protection and site operations and uses verified information in that work.

## Compare the work

| Compare | Intelligence analyst | Security manager |
|---|---|---|
| Focus here | Are the notices independent, and what can be confirmed? | Which operating arrangements need to change? |
| Output | Sourced briefing and unresolved questions | Decision, recipients, and a review time |

## The same event briefing

In a fictional public safety collaboration, an authorized liaison team asks an intelligence analyst to verify conflicting public-event notices before briefing the venue. Two messages trace back to the same outdated notice. The analyst verifies today's time against the organizer's latest announcement and flags tomorrow's schedule as unconfirmed. The venue's security manager updates reception and the shift lead for today, keeps tomorrow's arrangements in place, and sets a time to check again.

## Getting started in the U.S.

O*NET places both occupations in Job Zone Four, which indicates considerable preparation. An analyst candidate can show how they corroborated a report and documented gaps. A security manager candidate can show how they coordinated a change across shifts. Check the specific employer's data access, hiring, and duty requirements. ## Working conditions

Analysts need time to corroborate information and write, sometimes under briefing deadlines. Security managers may need to coordinate shifts and communicate changes as they happen. Ask who receives time-sensitive updates and how receipt is recorded.

## Moving between roles

Analysts can bring briefing and evidence evaluation into management and learn staffing and operations. Managers can bring incident context and reporting experience into analysis and build skills in corroboration and documenting uncertainty.

## Try both in 30 minutes

Use three fictional records. A 10 a.m. organizer notice says today's event starts at 3 p.m. Two 10:15 messages repeat yesterday's notice, which says 4 p.m. No notice covers tomorrow. Spend 10 minutes writing a briefing and its gap, 10 writing today's site update and who receives it, and 10 setting the condition for reviewing tomorrow's plan.
