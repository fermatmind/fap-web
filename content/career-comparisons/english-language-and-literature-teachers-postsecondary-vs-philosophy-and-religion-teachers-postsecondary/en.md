# Postsecondary English Teachers vs. Philosophy and Religion Teachers: Reading an Excuse

## What each role delivers

Postsecondary English language and literature teachers teach reading and writing and evaluate textual work. Philosophy and religion teachers teach subjects such as ethics, logic or religious studies and assess conceptual and written work.

## Compare the work

| Work item | English Language and Literature Teachers, Postsecondary | Philosophy and Religion Teachers, Postsecondary |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Close reading and writing feedback | Conceptual discussion and argument assessment |
| Basis | Wording and narrative evidence | Claims, premises and objections |
| U.S. median annual wage (2025) | $78,760 | $80,260 |

## How the work meets

In a fictional passage, a narrator excuses a broken promise. The English instructor asks students to cite wording that establishes tone. The philosophy instructor asks students to reconstruct the reasoning and identify a counterexample.

## How to enter

BLS lists a doctoral or professional degree as typical for both rows. Check the department’s field, course assignment and scholarship expectations. [BLS occupation rows](https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/occupational-projections-and-characteristics.htm)

## Work conditions

Both roles involve preparation and feedback. English courses may require iterative writing feedback; philosophy or religion courses may involve seminar and argument assessment. Ask how grading and advising count toward workload.

## Moving between roles

Moving to philosophy, bring close reading and develop disciplinary scholarship and argument teaching. Moving to English, bring structured feedback and build literary analysis and writing pedagogy.

## Try it in 30 minutes

Use a fictional three-sentence account of a broken promise. Spend ten minutes each drafting a close-reading assignment, an argument task and the evidence that would earn credit in each.
