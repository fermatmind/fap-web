# Electrical Engineer vs. Physicist

## Meeting a design requirement and explaining a result

An electrical engineer designs or evaluates equipment against requirements and documents how to verify it. A physicist studies a phenomenon, analyzes measurements, and explains what the evidence supports under the conditions tested. Both may contribute to the same experiment.

## Compare the work products

| Work | Electrical engineer | Physicist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First check | Whether the device meets a stated requirement | Whether the observation supports an explanation |
| Deliverable | A design recommendation and verification record | A comparison of hypotheses and the limits of the evidence |
| Handoff | Operating constraints and acceptance criteria | Comparable data and remaining evidence needs |

## A reading drifts as the temperature rises

A fictional team sees an offset in readings as a measurement module warms. The electrical engineer compares the records with the product’s accuracy requirement and flags conditions that may require a design review or operating limit. The physicist checks whether the same observations could reflect a change in the sample or a shift in the instrument baseline and proposes comparison data that could distinguish them. The team uses both findings to plan the next review.

## U.S. education and early experience

BLS describes a bachelor’s degree in electrical engineering or a related engineering field as typical. An internship or co-op can provide practice documenting a design and testing it against requirements. For physics research and academic roles, BLS describes a doctorate as typical. Entry-level federal physicist roles typically call for a bachelor’s degree in physics. A supervised lab or research project can help you build experience analyzing measurements and explaining uncertainty. [BLS engineering preparation](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/electrical-and-electronics-engineers.htm#tab-4) · [BLS physics preparation](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/life-physical-and-social-science/physicists-and-astronomers.htm#tab-4)

## Ask what sets the schedule

Electrical engineers may split their time between an office and project sites. Physicists may work in laboratories or offices, with travel or facility access depending on the research. Ask engineering teams when design reviews and verification occur, and which issues have to close before delivery. Ask physics teams who sets research questions, how instrument time is scheduled, and whether the work is expected to produce reports, papers, or technical deliverables. [BLS engineering work environment](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/electrical-and-electronics-engineers.htm#tab-3) · [BLS comparison work environment](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/life-physical-and-social-science/physicists-and-astronomers.htm#tab-3)

## Moving between the roles

An engineer can bring instrument experience and test records into research, then build skills in testing hypotheses and analyzing uncertainty. A physicist can bring modeling and measurement experience into engineering, then practice writing design requirements and choosing how to verify them.

## A 30-minute paper exercise

Use a fictional reference value of 10.0 and a maximum absolute error of 0.2. The room-temperature readings are 10.1 and 10.0, and the warmer readings are 10.3 and 10.4. Spend ten minutes flagging readings beyond the limit, ten writing separate sample-change and instrument-baseline explanations, and ten producing a verification note and a request for comparison data.
