# Middle-School CTE Teachers vs. Instructional Coordinators: Who Revises the Lesson?

## What each role delivers

Middle-school CTE teachers deliver technical lessons and adjust instruction based on student work. Instructional coordinators observe teaching, review curriculum materials and organize teacher development across classes or schools.

## Compare the work

| Work item | Career/Technical Education Teachers, Middle School | Instructional Coordinators |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Classroom instruction and feedback | Cross-class review and pilot proposal |
| Scope | Assigned students | Teachers, classes and curriculum leads |

## How the work meets

Three fictional classes use one project sheet. One class often leaves the roles box blank. The teacher revises that class’s demonstration. The coordinator compares materials and instruction across classes before proposing a small pilot to the curriculum lead.

## How to enter

BLS lists a bachelor’s degree and less than five years of related experience as typical for middle-school CTE teachers. For instructional coordinators, its occupation row lists a master’s degree and five years or more of related experience as typical. California’s Designated Subjects CTE credential is one example of authorization for named vocational subjects through grade 12. Check the state and school assignment. [California CTC CTE authorization](https://www.ctc.ca.gov/credentials/leaflets/cl-888/) [BLS occupation rows](https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/occupational-projections-and-characteristics.htm)

## Work conditions

Teachers work around classes and student feedback. Coordinators spend more time observing, meeting and planning professional development. Ask how recommendations become school decisions and whether the job includes teaching.

## Moving between roles

Moving into coordination, bring classroom evidence and learn cross-class review and teacher development. Moving to teaching, add direct classroom practice and relevant authorization.

## Try it in 30 minutes

Use mock records: A has six omissions out of ten, B has one, and C has none. Spend ten minutes on a classroom adjustment, ten on additional evidence, and ten drafting a limited pilot and what you would observe.
