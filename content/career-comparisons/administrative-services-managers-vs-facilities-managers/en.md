# Administrative Services Managers vs. Facilities Managers: Who Gets an Office Running Again?

## One keeps work moving; the other keeps the building usable

Administrative services managers organize support that employees use to get work done, such as office services, records, or supplies. Facilities managers coordinate the upkeep and repair of the physical workplace. Both may manage vendors, but an administrative services manager checks whether people can keep working; a facilities manager checks what the site needs before it can return to use.

## Compare the things each manager signs off on

| What to compare | Administrative services managers | Facilities managers |
|---|---|---|
| Main assignment | Office support, procedures, records, and service coordination | Space, building systems, maintenance, and repair coordination |
| Example output | Temporary workplace plan and staff notice | Repair work order, inspection record, and a recommendation on reopening |
| What can interrupt the day | Conflicting room, staffing, or service requests | A system fault or a delay that keeps a space closed |

See O*NET’s occupational tasks for [administrative services managers](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/11-3012.00#Tasks) and [facilities managers](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/11-3013.00#Tasks).

## One closed floor, two decisions

Imagine a water leak closes an office floor on Monday. The facilities manager works with the appropriate people to find the affected areas, arrange repair, and document the checks the organization requires before reopening. The administrative services manager uses that timeline to move meetings, arrange desks, and let staff and visitors know what will change. If inspection slips, facilities updates the reopening estimate and its basis; administrative services decides how to extend the temporary arrangements. The organization assigns the actual authority to reopen.

## Prepare for the work you want to manage

The [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/management/administrative-services-managers.htm#tab-4) says both jobs typically require a bachelor’s degree and related experience, with requirements varying by employer and assignment. For administrative services, look for chances to coordinate vendors, track office support requests, or revise a service procedure. For facilities, seek supervised experience with maintenance schedules, work orders, and the documents used to confirm repairs. Ask the employer which responsibilities it delegates and whether any specialized work is assigned to licensed staff or contractors.

## Ask what happens when plans change

Administrative coverage can draw you across several internal teams. Facilities work can include site checks and interruptions when repair or inspection timing changes. In administrative services, ask what happens when several teams need the same space or supplier at once and who can authorize extra spending. In facilities, ask who takes after-hours calls, handles contractor delays, and confirms that an area can reopen. If one position covers both, ask how it is staffed when an incident creates simultaneous demands.

## Show the part of your experience that transfers

To move from administrative services into facilities, show how you followed up on vendor deadlines, then add an example that tracks a work order from first report to verification. Moving the other way, show how you helped people keep working during planned maintenance. Explain the tradeoff you made between limited space, service continuity, and the time available to communicate updates.

## Try a 30-minute office closure exercise

In a fictional office the east wing closes. Five of twelve staff can work remotely and seven temporary desks are free. Two meetings start at 10. One can move online and one spare room is available. Spend ten minutes drafting staff arrangements, ten listing reopening checks, and ten revising both when inspection slips by a day. Leave reopening to the designated authority.
