# Precision Agriculture Technicians vs. Remote Sensing Technicians: A Dark Patch in a Field Image

## What each role delivers

Precision agriculture technicians combine yield maps, samples and field records to support site-specific management. Remote sensing technicians acquire and process imagery and check the data for quality and coverage.

## Compare the work

| Work item | Precision Agriculture Technicians | Remote Sensing Technicians |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Field follow-up locations and records | Image batch and quality notes |
| Basis | Yield, samples and field observations | Acquisition date, coverage and obscured areas |

## How the work meets

A fictional farm receives imagery with dark patches. The remote sensing technician masks an area of cloud shadow in the south. The precision agriculture technician uses field history and usable imagery to pick follow-up spots in the north and records which evidence is missing in the south.

## How to enter

In U.S. O*NET responses, 35% cited a bachelor’s degree and 30% cited a two-year degree for precision agriculture. For remote sensing technicians, 62% cited a bachelor’s degree. Ask employers what field or image processing experience they accept. ## Work conditions

Agricultural assignments may follow sampling and harvest windows. Image work may follow acquisition batches and quality reviews. Ask how much of each role is fieldwork.

## Moving between roles

To move into remote sensing, build image preparation and quality documentation on top of field sampling skills. To move into precision agriculture, add yield records, field checks and crop calendars.

## Try it in 30 minutes

Mock records show two historical samples in the north, cloud shadow in the south and imagery acquired two weeks before yield was recorded. Spend ten minutes each on an image quality note, a field follow-up list and a statement of what the current records support.
